Module 01
The Position
Before you build anything, you decide exactly what you sell and to whom. Get this wrong and everything after it is wasted effort.

Why most people never start

They have five ideas and commit to none. Every week a new one feels better than the last, so nothing gets finished. The problem isn't a shortage of ideas — it's the refusal to kill four of them.

A position is a decision you stop revisiting. Until you make it, you're not building a business, you're collecting options.

The Rule

You can change your position later. You cannot change it weekly. Pick one, commit for 90 days, then reassess with real data instead of feelings.

The four questions

1
What can you do that others find hard?
Not what you're world-class at — what you're simply further ahead on than the person you're selling to. That gap is enough.
2
Who specifically has this problem?
"Anyone who wants to make money" is not an audience. "People who've built a product but can't sell it" is.
3
What do they already pay to solve it?
If nobody spends money on this problem, it isn't painful enough. Existing spending is proof of demand.
4
Can you deliver it without permission?
No licence, no team, no big budget. If you need any of those to start, pick something else for now.

Narrow beats broad — always

Broad positioning feels safer because it seems like a bigger market. In reality it makes you invisible. Nobody searches for "business help." They search for the exact thing they're stuck on.

  • Broad: "I help people with business" → competes with everyone, chosen by no one
  • Narrow: "I help operators price and sell their first digital product" → obvious, specific, chooseable
  • The test: can someone describe what you do in one sentence after seeing your page once?
Do This Now

Write one sentence: "I help [specific person] to [specific outcome] without [common obstacle]." That sentence is your position. Every piece of content, every DM, and every product decision flows from it. Test the sentence on five people in that market and rewrite any words they do not immediately understand.

Module 02
The Build
How to make something premium fast — without a team, a budget, or six months of your life.

Speed is the advantage

Most people spend months building the perfect product and never sell it. The operator builds a strong version in days, sells it, then improves it with real buyer feedback. Perfection before revenue is procrastination wearing a smart outfit.

The Principle

Ship something genuinely good, not something half-finished. The difference between "fast" and "sloppy" is scope — a small thing done excellently beats a large thing done badly.

What makes a product feel premium

  • Depth over length. Ten pages that solve the problem completely beat a hundred pages of padding.
  • Design signals value. The same information in a beautiful format is worth multiples of the same information in a plain doc.
  • Specificity. Real numbers, worked examples, exact scripts. Vague advice feels cheap because it is.
  • Usability. Templates and tools they can actually use beat theory they have to translate.

The build sequence

1
Outline the transformation
Where does the buyer start, where do they end up? Every section moves them one step along that path.
2
Write the spine first
Core content only, no polish. Get the full argument down before you make anything look good.
3
Add the assets
Templates, scripts, checklists — the things they'll actually use. This is what justifies premium pricing.
4
Design it last
Never design an empty shell. Content first, then make it look like it costs what you're charging.
Do This Now

Write a one-page product brief containing the buyer, starting problem, final outcome, delivery format, included assets and build order. Then use the Price Engine to create a starting-price hypothesis before validating it with real buyers.

Module 03
The Store
A simple sales setup you control, with clear payment, delivery and support responsibilities.

Platforms take more than you think

Marketplaces and payment providers have different fees, rules and levels of control. Choose deliberately and include the real processing, refund and support costs in your pricing.

The alternative is simpler than people assume: a page that explains the offer, a way to take payment, and a way to deliver. That's a store.

The minimum viable sales system

1
A page that sells
Problem, solution, what's inside, price, objections, call to action. One page, hosted free.
2
A secure way to get paid
Use a reputable checkout or payment provider, disclose the price clearly, and account for processing fees and refunds.
3
A way to deliver
A private link plus an access code. Sent manually at first — automation comes once volume justifies it.
4
Proof, added over time
Every buyer is asked for one line of feedback. That line goes on the page. Proof compounds.
The Trap

Don't build sophisticated automation before you've made a sale. Manual delivery is completely fine for your first twenty customers — and it lets you talk to every single one, which is worth more than the time it saves.

Pricing without flinching

  • Price on outcome, not effort. How long it took you is irrelevant to what it's worth to them.
  • Cheap creates doubt. Under-pricing signals low value and attracts the worst customers.
  • Your price is a message. It tells the buyer what kind of thing this is before they read a word.
  • Raise it for new buyers. Existing customers keep their price. New ones pay the new one.
Module 04
The Signal
Getting attention consistently — faceless, from zero, without needing anything to go viral.

Attention is earned by consistency, not luck

Virality is not a strategy because you can't control it. What you can control is whether you show up. The accounts that win aren't the luckiest — they're the ones that didn't stop.

The Reality

Almost everyone posts to near-silence at first. The people you see winning now went through exactly that. The difference is they kept going long enough for it to compound.

The faceless formats that work

  • Text on screen. A message over a simple background. Fast to make, easy to batch, endlessly repeatable.
  • Screen recordings. Showing a process or result. Builds credibility because you're demonstrating, not claiming.
  • Voiceover and footage. Carries personality without showing your face.
  • Product-led. The work itself is the content. You stay behind the camera entirely.

The hook is the whole game

You have about one second. The best content in the world dies behind a weak opening. Lead with the most interesting thing you've got — never warm up, never introduce.

  • Be specific. "How I made money" is ignorable. "The one message that got me my first client" is not.
  • Open a loop. Make them need the answer. "The mistake that cost me everything" demands the rest.
  • Take a position. Bland content gets scrolled past. A clear stance gets remembered.

A rhythm you can actually keep

Posting once a day forever beats five times a day for a week then quitting. Choose the pace you can sustain on your worst week, not your best. Batch your content so posting doesn't depend on daily motivation.

Module 05
The Close
Turning strangers into buyers through direct outreach — the fastest path to revenue that exists.

Outreach is a service, not begging

Most people avoid DMs because they feel like an imposition. That framing is the problem. If what you're offering genuinely helps them, reaching out is a service. The discomfort comes from selling something you don't believe in — or from not being clear on the value.

Why This Beats Content Early On

Content needs volume and time to work. Outreach works from message one. When you need revenue now, you don't post and wait — you talk to people directly.

The four-part message

1
Open with them, not you
Reference something specific and real about their situation. Generic openers get ignored instantly.
2
Name the problem you solve
One sentence. Show you understand what they're dealing with better than they expected.
3
Offer proof, not claims
Show the work. A link, an example, a result. Proof does the persuading so you don't have to.
4
Make a small ask
Not "buy now." Ask a question that starts a conversation. The sale comes from the conversation.

The numbers to expect

  • Messages to replies: a meaningful minority reply. Most won't. That's normal, not failure.
  • Replies to conversations: roughly half go somewhere if your opener was relevant.
  • Conversations to sales: a solid fraction convert when the offer fits.
  • The lesson: volume solves everything. Rejection is arithmetic, not judgement.
The Follow-Up Nobody Does

Most sales happen after the second or third message, and most people send exactly one. A short, non-pushy follow-up a few days later converts more than any clever opening line.

Module 06
The Machine
Turning scattered effort into a system that runs without you restarting from scratch every week.

The gap between busy and building

Most people work hard in bursts and wonder why nothing compounds. A machine is what happens when the same actions get repeated on a schedule, whether you feel like it or not.

The weekly operating rhythm

1
Batch your content once
One session produces a week or two of posts. Posting stops competing with building.
2
Set an outreach number
A fixed count of conversations started per day. Non-negotiable, tracked, done before anything else.
3
Improve the product with feedback
Every buyer teaches you something. The product gets better and the price justification gets stronger.
4
Track only what matters
Messages sent, conversations started, sales closed. Three numbers. Everything else is noise.
The Honest Warning

The most common failure isn't building a bad product — it's building a good one and never consistently selling it. Building feels productive and safe. Selling feels exposing. If you notice yourself starting a new build right before the selling step, that's the pattern. Name it and go sell instead.

What compounds

  • Proof. Each customer becomes a testimonial that makes the next sale easier.
  • Content. Every post stays out there working long after you publish it.
  • Skill. The tenth sales conversation is dramatically better than the first.
  • Assets. Products you've built keep selling with no additional work.

Objection Handlers

"It's too expensive"
Completely fair. Worth asking though — what's it currently costing you to leave [PROBLEM] as it is?

Most people find the answer is more than the price, which is usually the point at which it makes sense.
"I need to think about it"
Of course — makes sense to be sure.

Just so I can help properly: is it the price, the timing, or are you not convinced it'll work for your situation? Happy to be straight with you either way.
"I could do this myself"
You absolutely could — it's not secret information.

The question is whether you want to spend [TIME] figuring it out, or start from a system that already works. Both are valid, depends what your time's worth right now.
"Does it actually work?"
Honest answer: it works if you run it. It's not magic — it's a system, and systems need someone to execute them.

What I can show you is [PROOF/EXAMPLE]. What I can't do is do it for you.
